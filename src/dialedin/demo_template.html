Dialed In

We built a measurable control over a language model's internal frequency response, isolated a major contributor to that control, and ran the experiments through an AI scientist loop that tested its own proposals. A slow test signal passes through Muse Glimmer-30B's attention heads more strongly than a fast one. We call that the lean. A small trained dial turns it down, about half of the dial sits in one matrix, and Muse proposes and reviews what to test next.

1 · The dial

Turn the lean down

We add a faint sine wave with a period of N tokens to the model's hidden states and read how strongly each head carries it. Gain climbs with the period: slow test signals get through more strongly than fast ones. This is a measurement with an injected probe; whether the model also favors long-range meaning in ordinary text is not yet shown. The dial is a small trained adapter, and flatter is not better: it is a control, with a small cost. Each setting below was measured on five books.

Orange: the selected strength. Dashed blue: the dial at 0. Thin gray: the other measured strengths. Each line is one setting; the slider picks which one is drawn in orange.

2 · Where the dial acts

One matrix carries about half of the dial

Each cell is one attention head, colored by its own lean; the lean is spread across many layers. The adapter touches layers 2 to 5. Switching its parts on and off located where the dial acts: three matrices in layer 2 carry most of its effect, and one of them, layer 2's v, about half. This locates the dial, not the origin of the lean.

3 · The lab loop

Muse as the scientist

Muse Spark narrates results and critiques designs. Muse Wonder proposes the next experiment and reviews the code. A person seals each pre-registered spec before the GPU runs it. Every number Muse writes must match a cited fact from the result files.
